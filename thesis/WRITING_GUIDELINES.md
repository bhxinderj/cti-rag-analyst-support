# Writing Guidelines

## Purpose
Use these guidelines for all thesis-related writing in this project.

## Core style
Write in clear, formal academic English.
Aim for precision, not verbosity.
Keep the tone analytical, restrained, and professional.

## Language conventions
- American English throughout (analyze, behavior, modeling).
- No first person ("I", "we"). Use "this thesis", "the prototype", "the evaluation".
- Present tense for system description and facts ("the retriever combines ...").
- Past tense for what was done ("the evaluation was conducted ...", Chapter 4).

## Principles
- State the point early.
- Prefer specific claims over broad statements.
- Make the logic easy to follow.
- Keep sentences reasonably short.
- Use technical terms only where they add value.
- Avoid repetition.
- Cut anything that does not support the argument.

## What to avoid
- Inflated wording
- Generic filler
- Obvious AI-style phrasing
- Empty transitions
- Overstated claims
- Marketing language
- Long paragraphs that mix multiple ideas

## AI-tells (concrete list)
- Words: leverage, delve, robust, seamless, comprehensive, crucial, notably,
  pivotal, landscape (figurative), harness, empower, foster.
- Chains of "furthermore / moreover / additionally".
- "not only ... but also".
- Constant rule-of-three enumerations ("X, Y, and Z" in every second sentence).
- Consecutive paragraphs with identical structure.
- Summary sentences that add nothing ("In summary, this shows ...").
- Excessive em-dashes; prefer commas or parentheses.

## Preferred writing pattern
For most paragraphs:
1. Start with the main claim or observation.
2. Support it with reasoning, evidence, or literature.
3. End with the implication only if it matters.

## Argumentation
- Every section should have a clear purpose.
- Research questions, methods, and results must align.
- Do not promise more than the thesis actually delivers.
- Make limitations explicit instead of hiding them.
- Distinguish clearly between established findings, design decisions, and interpretation.

## Academic tone
Prefer:
- "This thesis examines ..."
- "The results indicate ..."
- "A limitation of this approach is ..."
- "The prototype is intended to ..."
- "The evaluation focuses on ..."

Avoid:
- "This groundbreaking thesis ..."
- "It is very important to note ..."
- "In today's rapidly evolving world ..."
- "This clearly proves ..."
- "It cannot be denied that ..."

## Style rules
- Prefer active voice when it improves clarity.
- Use passive voice only when the actor is irrelevant (common and fine in Methodology).
- Avoid rhetorical questions.
- Avoid conversational phrases.
- Avoid vague intensifiers such as "very", "extremely", or "highly" unless justified.
- Use consistent terminology throughout the document.

## Terminology canon
Always the same term for the same thing (extend this list while writing):
- "the prototype" (not "the system" / "the tool" interchangeably)
- "hybrid retrieval" (BM25 + dense vector search)
- "reranking" (not "re-ranking")
- "query set" (not "test set" for the evaluation queries)
- "non-retrieval baseline" (configuration V0)
- IDs: configurations V0–V5, design criteria C1–C6, bottlenecks B1–B3, gaps G1–G3
- "threat hunting" (noun), "threat-hunting workflows" (adjective)

## Paragraph discipline
- One main idea per paragraph.
- Split paragraphs that become too broad.
- Do not stack multiple claims without support.
- Keep transitions functional and minimal.

## Evidence and claims
- Ground factual or evaluative claims in sources, data, or explicit reasoning.
- Do not overgeneralize from limited prototype results.
- Be careful with words such as "proves", "demonstrates", or "validates".
- Prefer measured formulations such as "suggests", "indicates", or "supports".
- Avoid double hedging ("may potentially suggest").

## Thesis-specific emphasis
The writing should reflect:
- practical relevance for CTI / security analyst support
- methodological clarity
- realistic scope
- explicit grounding of conclusions in the prototype and evaluation

## Revision checklist
Before finalizing a section, check:
- Is the wording precise?
- Is the paragraph necessary?
- Is the argument easy to follow?
- Does this sound like a human academic writer rather than generated filler?
- Can any sentence be shortened without losing meaning?
